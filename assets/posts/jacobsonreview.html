A Review of Jacobson's Basic Algebra 1.

This is a wonderful book in its own way. It assumes nothing and builds up to great heights, even dedicating time to mathematical induction and proving results such as the fundamental theorem of arithmetic in chapter 0. Though you would be mistaken for thinking that it is unsophisticated, in fact it is the complete opposite. In chapter 0, a few pages after telling the reader what a set is, it proves that recursively defined functions on $\mathbb{N}$ exist and are unique. This was a shock to my system and many hours were spent browsing MathStackExchange looking for an explanation of the proof. This pattern continues throughout the book.


We move somewhat quickly from very basic notions to significantly more advanced ones without taking time to stop and admire the view. For instance, we prove the Sylow theorems in an unorthodox way, the standard proof being given as a series of exercises. Or, partway through our discussion of monoids, we prove Cayley's theorem.


The exercises in these book are absolutely fantastic. They range from insightful to mindblowing. A particular highlight for me was showing that primes congruent to 1 modulo 4 are always the sum of two squares. In fact, I can probably trace my love of algebraic number theory back to doing this very exercise. There is not a single boring exercise in this book.


As the exercises aren't boring, it also means that the reader is expected to do the work in understanding the material. They are expected to come up with their own examples, do their own computations and experiment with the material. Jacobson is treating the reader as an adult, and offering the dessert of interesting exercises knowing that the reader has already eaten their computational vegetables. If you don't, however, this book is destined to do you more harm than good. I suspect that this is what happened to me at one point, I simply moved through the material too quickly, wasting months of reading time.


A quick note should also be made about the exposition. Jacobson's affinity for algebra is evident - his explanations are lucid and clear. However, it can be old school at times. It was first published in 1974, however Jacobson received his PhD under Joseph Wedderburn (the same one as in the Artin-Wedderburn theorem!) in 1934. As a result, there is an old-school tinge, and there are some things mentioned that I haven't seen elsewhere. For example, the exponent of a group is used to prove that finite subgroups of the multiplicative group of a field are cyclic. (As far as I understand, this proof is somewhat nonstandard, and we typically use the structure theorem.)


Overall, Basic Algebra I is spectacular but requires a significant amount of mathematical maturity and some mathematical backgroud. It is at minimum readable after having finishing Algebra II at Warwick, but I would wait a little longer to make sure one gets the most out of it.
