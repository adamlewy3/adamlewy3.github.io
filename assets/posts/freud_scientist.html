In this post, we discuss the question of whether Freud was a scientist. We begin by considering a toy model of scientific discovery, which allows us to isolate two potential prerequisites for discovery - appropriate concepts and mental models. Then, we claim that asking whether Freud was a scientist is missing the mark. Freud's contribution is in isolating the relevant concepts, asking the correct questions and giving a corpus of work on which we can build.

Was Freud a Scientist?

In order to answer the titular question, we must ask what is meant by 'scientist', and thus 'science'. This is a difficult question, and we don't even attempt answer it. For now, we understand science to be some sort of rigorous theory-crafting whose goal is to explain some system. By system we mean something which has a function and components (Ritchey 1991). As a concrete example, suppose I am interested in understanding astigmatism. This is a phenomenon that arises from the eye. The eye is the system, (one of) its functions is seeing, and it has many components, such as an iris, lens, cornea, rods, cones, an optic nerve and so on.

The components of Scientific Discovery

We split up Scientific Discovery into three components (Schickore 2025):


  • Knowledge Generation
  • Elucidation
  • Assessment

The first phase, knowledge generation, concerns itself with the purported 'Eureka' moment that comes from finding a concept or hypothesis. This is the most preliminary phase of investigating a system. Elucidation concerns itself with explaining a theory or hypothesis in detail, and assessment is self-explanatory.

How to Investigate a System: a toy model of Science.

As mentioned prior, the system concept consists of a function and its components. Thus, we may investigate the system by investigating these in turn. If we investigate purely the function, we say that we are treating the system as a black-box. Alternatively, we may break the system down into its components, investigate each component in turn, and investigate the relationship between each component.


Now that we know what questions we can ask about the system, we still need to know how to investigate a system. This naturally splits into (at least) two approaches - analysis and synthesis. This distinction goes back to the ancient Greeks and has been a mainstay in intellectual history. Leibniz defines analysis and synthesis as follows:


"Synthesis ... is the process in which we begin from principles and [proceed to] build up theorems and problems, ... while analysis is the process in which we begin with a given conclusion or proposed problem and seek the principles by which we may demonstrate the conclusion or solve the problem." (Ritchey 1991, citing Leibniz)


Analysis and synthesis are ways of going through the three steps of scientific inquiry, as the following example will explain.


Consider an analysis of the components of the eye. I begin with the hypothesis that the eye is responsible for seeing, and thus must have a mechanism for detecting light. I look at an eye through an electron microscope and discover the photo-receptors (rods and cones) on the retina. This is knowledge generation. Thus, I synthesise.


In the eye, there are rods and cones, which are responsible for detecting light.


This is also elucidation. I may publish my findings in a paper, and then the scientific community will scrutinise my findings. This is assessment. Then the process restarts.


It is not very clear what the purpose of the above synthesis is. It feels as if we have simply restated the conclusions of the analysis. Whether there is a distinction between synthesis and analysis, and what these terms have meant to historical figures who used them is, as far as I understand it, an open question (Koertge 1980). Regardless, it is undoubtedly the case that the above synthesis serves a cognitive purpose. The neat summary of my discovery allows me to ask further hypothetical questions, make predictions, and do more analysis - as the next paragraphs hopefully demonstrate.


I recall that, upon waking, I feel more energetic after seeing sunlight. So, I begin with the hypothesis that there is more to the eye than just seeing - it is also responsible for detecting sunlight, and in particular for the circadian rhythm. Thus, I predict that there exist specific sensors in the eye which respond to 'early-morning light'. Indeed, such sensors exist - they are called intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells or ipRGCs. By analysing function, I have learned something new about the components. The analyses of function and components go hand in hand.


Similarly, analysis and synthesis go hand in hand. Based on my knowledge of the various sensors in the eye, I attempt to reconstruct the eye from what I know, and the 'principles' which guide the eye. For instance, I may begin with the following hypothesis:


Rods, cones and ipRGCs are responsible for detecting light.


From this, I come to the following conclusion:


As astigmatism is a failure to detect light correctly, astigmatism is caused by a disorder of the rods, cones or ipRGCs.


This ends up not being correct. Astigmatism is caused by a slightly misshapen lens, rather than any failure with the photoreceptors. However, this example demonstrate the iterative procedure that is common in science.


What precedes the eye-myth?

The above is blatant myth creation on my end. It is certainly not the case that this is how the photoreceptors were discovered. Moreover, the black-boxing of analysis leaves a lot of questions unanswered, the most pertinent being:


What motivates us to ask the correct question?


I will answer this with a story. When I first began to write this post, I didn't know about the existence of ipRGC's. I really did recall that I felt more energised in the morning, and I really did ask whether there were different photoreceptors responsible for this. How is this possible when I don't know any explicit details about how eyes work, I've never used an electron microscope and I didn't do biology past the age of 16?


The answer is because I had an appropriate mental model of the eye - a schematic in my head about how the eye works. It goes somewhat like this:


Light enters the eye through the pupil, the photoreceptors receive this light energy, which is then converted into electrical energy that travels along the optic nerve into the brain, which processes that information and produces an image, or some other response.


However, this mental model crucially relies on the fact that the important concepts, such as the photo-receptors, have already been found by other scientists.


A Moderate Defence of Freud

It is not immediately clear what function the human psyche plays, what its limits are or what components it has. In the above, we showed how using a mental model can circumvent some of these issues - the appropriate mental model of the eye allowed us to discover a component. Observation allowed us to discover a function.


Freud's work gave us a plethora of components/concepts and mental models on which we could begin to analyse the psyche. He recognised that fundamentally new tools are required to investigate such a system, much in the same way that Riemann recognised that Helmholtz's methodology of investigating the ear is fundamentally flawed (Ritchey 1991), and that something fundamentally different was needed.

Among the concepts, observations and models that were gifted to us by Freud, I would like to emphasise the following:


  • The existence of unconscious mental processes, in addition to other unconscious states, such as (procedural) memory.
  • The ubiquity of the unconscious - in particular affective and motivational unconscious processes.
  • The recognition of psychical defence.
  • The recognition of repression.
    • The importance of one's own mental representation on their lives/experiences of others - more broadly, the shift from content of thoughts to the way in which we think. The conception of mental development as moving from immaturity to maturity (in a precise sense). The observation of conclict between conscious and unconscious, especially in cases such as binge-eating and depression. The importance and prevalence of sexuality. The recognition of transference.

The above are still found in modern psychodynamic (read psychoanalytic) therapy and are enjoying increasing empirical support and acceptance in psychology (Westen 1998). I also find them personally valuable when thinking about myself and the world around me, and they will be explored in future posts.


Conclusion: Was Freud a Scientist?

We have very good reason to say that Freud wasn't a scientist. There are definitely issues in Freud's work as regards the 'assessment' phase of scientific discovery.


If one understands 'assessment' to mean 'reproducible by other scientists', then one will find issue with Freud. The epistemic basis of much of his work is, essentially, 'I once had a patient'. This is, of course, difficult to reproduce.


If one understands 'assessment' to mean 'falsifiable' (as Popper does), then one will be left equally unsatisfied. One of the most egregious examples of unfalsifiability I've found in Freud is his assertion that 'every dream is the fulfillment of a wish' (Freud 1900a). Freud isn't even pretending that you can disprove this theory!


There are also genuine questions of whether Freud uses logically valid arguments in his work. For instance, in The Unconscious, (Freud 1915e) Freud argues for the existence of the unconscious abductively (inference to best explanation).


  1. There are unexplained phenomena $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$.
  2. The phenomena $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$ would be explained by the hypothesis $H$ (in this case, the existence of unconscious mental processes).
  3. Therefore $H$.

In my opinion, Freud does not justify $H$ well enough. He does not do enough to exclude other possibilities, but this is something we will explore another time.


There is also a very real question of whether we ought to accept a concept because it has been effective in clinical work.


That said, there are certainly ways in which Freud was a good scientist. He relied heavily on the work that came before him, e.g. the first chapter of the Interpretation of Dreams (Freud 1900a) is a literature review. He collected lots of data, his case studies are extensive. He was not a lone worker, being engaged with other academics, and he was well-versed in the standard of science at the time - his theories were sometimes extensions of accepted physical theories, for instance the theory of mental instinct (Freud 1915c). He was also a prolific and lucid writer, which is the second of the three components of scientific discovery we identified.


With all that said, levying criticisms of pseudo-science at Freud remains a category error. We have already seen how having the appropriate concepts can lead us to discovery (of ipRGC's in our case), and it is also clear that the incorrect conjecture we proposed shouldn't lead us to discard our mental model of the eye, or put into question the existence of the retina. To say that Freud's work is pseudo-scientific is a little like saying that Thales' theory that 'water is the origin of all things' is pseudo-scientific. It may be, but that is not the point. Today we recognise Thales as an important figure in intellectual history for even thinking about such a question, for believing that there is something beyond myth and for giving his successors a body of work on which to build.


So, in spite of Freud's periodic obituaries in popular media, there is good reason why we should not forget his name.


"Even when Freud had the wrong answer to a question, or refused to give an answer, he knew what the question was and what was at stake in it." (Glymour 1991)
